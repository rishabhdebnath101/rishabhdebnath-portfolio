Keyword Research in the Age of AI: How to Find Topics That Rank on Google and Get Cited by ChatGPT

Keyword research has always been the starting point of any SEO strategy — but AI search tools have changed what you are actually looking for. This article explains how modern keyword research works for both Google rankings and AI citation readiness, with practical steps you can apply right now.

RD
Rishabh Debnath SEO & GEO Specialist

8 min read

Key Takeaways

  • Topics over keywords: Modern keyword research means identifying topic clusters — groups of related questions — rather than chasing individual search terms with high volume.
  • Intent is the filter: Search intent (informational, navigational, commercial, transactional) determines whether a page should be an article, a landing page, or a comparison guide. Matching the wrong format to a keyword reduces ranking potential significantly.
  • AI tools favor question-rich content: ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews preferentially cite pages that directly answer specific questions with structured, factual prose — a pattern that also improves traditional Google rankings.
  • Free tools are enough to start: Google Search Console, People Also Ask boxes, and Google autocomplete reveal real user demand without paid subscriptions.
  • Topical authority compounds: Publishing multiple connected articles on related subtopics signals depth of expertise to search engines and strengthens citation probability across your entire content cluster.

1. What Keyword Research Actually Means in 2026

Keyword research is the process of identifying the words, phrases, and questions real people type into search engines — and using that information to decide what content to create. Traditionally, this meant finding terms with high monthly search volume and manageable competition, then optimizing individual pages around those terms.

That model still works, but it is no longer complete. AI-powered search tools — including Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT Search, and Perplexity — do not just rank documents. They retrieve passages, summarize information, and cite sources. This means keyword research now has two jobs: find topics that can earn organic Google rankings, and find topics where your content can serve as the factual source an AI cites in its answer.

Core Concept: Topics vs. Keywords

A keyword is a specific search phrase (e.g., "keyword research tools"). A topic is the broader subject area it belongs to (e.g., "how to do keyword research for SEO"). Effective modern keyword research works at the topic level first, then identifies the specific keyword variations to target within each topic.

2. The Four Types of Search Intent (And Why They Change Everything)

Search intent is the underlying goal behind a user's query. Google has become very good at identifying intent and matching the type of result to what the user actually wants. If your page format does not match the intent, it will struggle to rank — regardless of how well it is optimized technically.

Understanding intent is also important for AI citation. Perplexity and ChatGPT Search are more likely to cite informational content that directly answers a question than product pages or category listings.

Intent Type What the User Wants Best Content Format AI Citation Potential
Informational Learn about a topic or answer a question Blog article, guide, explainer High — AI tools preferentially cite factual explanations
Navigational Find a specific website or page Homepage, brand page Low — AI tools rarely cite navigational results
Commercial Research before making a purchase decision Comparison article, review, case study Medium — cited when structured comparisons are clear
Transactional Buy, sign up, or take action Product page, landing page Low — AI tools do not typically cite purchase pages

For most blog content targeting informational intent, the goal is to answer the question completely and clearly. For AI citation specifically, the most retrievable content tends to lead with a direct, factual answer in the first paragraph of each section — following the same inverted pyramid structure used in journalism.

3. How to Find Topics That Rank on Google and Get Cited by AI

The practical process of finding good topics combines traditional keyword data with an understanding of how AI retrieval systems select sources. Here is a step-by-step approach that works without expensive tools.

Step 1: Start with Google Search Console

Google Search Console (GSC) shows you the exact queries your website already appears for in Google Search — including queries on page 2 and 3 where small improvements could move you to page 1. Filter by queries with 100 to 1,000 impressions and a click-through rate below 5%. These are topics where Google has already decided your content is relevant, but your page is not yet ranking high enough to earn clicks.

Step 2: Mine People Also Ask and Autocomplete

Google's People Also Ask (PAA) boxes and autocomplete suggestions are direct windows into real user questions. When you type a seed keyword like "keyword research" into Google, the autocomplete suggestions reveal the most common ways people extend that query. The PAA boxes reveal the follow-up questions users ask most often — these are the subtopics you need to address to build topical coverage around your main topic.

Step 3: Test the Topic in AI Tools

Ask ChatGPT or Perplexity the core question you want to rank for. Notice which sources they cite and what format those sources use. If AI tools generate vague, generic answers that do not cite anyone specific, that signals an opportunity to create a well-structured, citable resource that fills the gap.

Step 4: Evaluate with Free Keyword Tools

Once you have a list of candidate topics, use free tools to validate demand and competition. Ahrefs Webmaster Tools offers keyword difficulty and volume data for your own site's queries at no cost. Google Trends helps identify whether a topic's interest is growing, stable, or declining — useful for deciding whether to prioritize it now or later.

4. Building Topical Authority Through Content Clusters

Topical authority refers to the degree to which a website is recognized as a comprehensive, reliable source on a subject. Search engines and AI retrieval systems are both more likely to rank and cite sites that demonstrate depth of coverage — not just sites with a single strong article.

A content cluster is a structured group of articles covering all the major subtopics within a broader theme. It typically consists of a pillar page supported by several cluster articles that dive deeper into specific subtopics. For example, a pillar page on SEO fundamentals might link to cluster articles on keyword research, technical SEO audits, and entity-based SEO.

Practical Note: Cluster Size

You do not need to publish 50 articles at once. A cluster of 5 to 8 tightly connected articles on related subtopics can begin building topical authority signals. Start with the most searched subtopics and expand outward based on what your Search Console data shows people are asking.

5. Common Keyword Research Mistakes That Hurt Both Google and AI Visibility

Understanding what to do is only half the picture. Avoiding these common mistakes tends to produce faster results than chasing tactics alone.

  • Targeting keywords without matching intent: Creating a product page for a query that users want answered as a how-to guide makes it structurally difficult for the page to rank well. Always verify the type of content Google already shows for your target keyword before deciding on your page format.
  • Ignoring long-tail and question-form queries: High-volume head terms are competitive and slow to move. Question-based queries are lower volume individually but collectively cover enormous ground — and they match exactly how AI tools phrase their internal retrieval queries.
  • Publishing isolated articles without internal links: A standalone article that links to nothing and receives no internal links is a dead-end for both crawlers and AI citation systems. Every new article should be connected to at least two or three related pages on your site.
  • Focusing only on volume and ignoring relevance: A keyword with 10,000 monthly searches is worthless if it has no connection to what you do or who you serve. Relevance to your subject matter expertise is a stronger selection criterion than raw volume.
  • Not updating underperforming pages: Keyword research is not a one-time event. Pages that plateau in rankings often benefit from a content refresh — adding new subtopics, updating examples, improving the answer structure.

6. A Practical Keyword Research Workflow

Putting the above steps together, here is a repeatable workflow for identifying topics that support both Google rankings and AI citation visibility.

  1. Define your core topic area: Identify the 2 to 3 broad subject areas most relevant to your work. Everything you publish should connect to at least one of these.
  2. Audit what you already rank for: Pull your GSC data and identify pages with rankings on page 2 or 3. These are your fastest improvement opportunities.
  3. Map your topic cluster: For each core subject area, brainstorm 8 to 12 subtopics using autocomplete, PAA, and competitor content as inputs. Group them by intent type.
  4. Prioritize by intent and gap: Focus first on informational subtopics where no strong, current resource exists. These have the highest AI citation potential and fastest ranking movement.
  5. Draft with structure in mind: Lead every section with a direct answer. Use headers that mirror the questions people actually ask. Include comparison tables where multiple options exist.
  6. Connect everything: After publishing, update older related articles to include a contextual link to the new piece. This connects the cluster and passes authority through internal links.

Frequently Asked Questions

Keyword research for AI search means identifying topics — not just individual keywords — that match what real users ask conversational AI tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews. Instead of targeting isolated search terms, the goal is to map clusters of related questions and subtopics so your content can be retrieved as a complete, authoritative answer by both traditional search engines and LLM-powered tools.

Traditional SEO keyword research focuses on search volume and keyword difficulty to choose individual terms to target. GEO keyword research adds a second layer: identifying topics where your content can serve as a citable, self-contained source for AI retrieval systems. This means prioritizing question-based queries, entity-rich topics, and comparison searches that AI tools are likely to summarize — even if monthly search volume is relatively modest.

Google Search Console is the most reliable free tool — it shows the exact queries your pages already rank for. Google's People Also Ask boxes and autocomplete suggestions reveal real question intent. Ahrefs Webmaster Tools offers a free tier for site-level keyword data. Google Trends helps evaluate topic momentum over time. For AI-specific research, manually testing questions in ChatGPT and Perplexity shows what kind of answers these tools generate — and whether your content could serve as a source.

Topical authority refers to the depth and breadth of coverage your website has on a specific subject area. Search engines and AI tools are more likely to cite sources that comprehensively cover a topic from multiple angles — not just a single article. Building topical authority means researching and publishing content across the full range of subtopics within your niche, connected through logical internal links, so Google and LLMs recognize your site as a reliable reference on that subject.
